1 · The pane now shows two things, not one fraction

You said: "puts traded and calls traded side by side would be pretty, pretty great. Which moved would be pretty great." And: "my brain has a little problem with these fractions because there's multiple inputs that can change to move the fraction."

So the pane draws the two inputs. Puts traded, calls traded, each as a bar, the published ratio above them, and one sentence naming the side that moved.

The fraction, once, in plain words

A lower number means fewer puts per call. 0.5 is one put for every two calls. 1.0 is one put for every call. 1.2 is twelve puts for every ten calls. Tonight's equity reading of 0.46 is about five puts for every ten calls.

The ratio can move for two different reasons, and they mean different things: puts being bought (people paying for protection) or calls being sold or simply not traded (the upside bet cooling). The two bars tell you which one happened; the fraction on its own cannot.

Where the two amounts come from. This is the part M50 could not find: Cboe already publishes them. The same daily file that carries the ratio carries the product's call volume and put volume. Read from Cboe's 2026-09-23 file: equity calls 2,647,370, equity puts 1,205,941 — which divide to 0.4555, and Cboe printed 0.46. The chart API was reading those two numbers to check the ratio and then throwing them away. Now it keeps them and serves them on the same candle, so the pane needs no new data source, no second request, and nothing from Interactive Brokers.

How to read the p. Neighbouring days overlap almost completely — the sixty days after a Tuesday are nearly the sixty days after the Monday before it — so counting them as separate facts would make almost anything look significant. Instead the same number of runs of consecutive days is drawn at random from the whole history, thousands of times, and p is the share of those random draws that beat the real one. A small p means the event days genuinely did better than chance; a large p means they did worse than an average day.

The honest verdict

A wide gap tells you nothing about what comes next. At every width — the median, the widest tenth, the widest 1% — SPY's next five, twenty and sixty sessions were indistinguishable from an ordinary day, and often slightly worse. The widest 1% of days (54 of them) were followed by -0.10% over five sessions against +0.20% for any day. So "index put/call is far above equity put/call" is not a signal to trade on.

The one band that stands out is the opposite one: on the 267 days when the gap was narrowest (5% of history), SPY's next sixty sessions averaged +3.52% against +2.36% for any day, and only 2% of random draws did better. Treat that as interesting, not proven: eight bands times three horizons is twenty-four measurements, and at this strength roughly one of them would look this good by luck alone. It is worth watching, not betting.

4 · The market-wide estimate: 500 or 1,000 names

You asked: "how do we measure 500 to 1,000 most traded option stocks for intraday? And what do we do about that?" Here is the plain answer.

Interactive Brokers gives one running call-volume and put-volume figure per underlying, and your account holds 101 lines open at once (measured, not assumed — IBKR refused the 102nd). So the reader subscribes to a batch, reads it, cancels it, and moves on. One full pass of 364 names took 121 seconds when the coordinator measured it.

How the names get chosen. Rank by the reader's own measured option volume over the first few sessions it runs, and keep the busiest 500. That is a list Scintilla owns and can re-rank weekly; it needs no outside list and no new subscription. Until enough sessions exist to rank with, the current 364 plus the index funds stand in, and the page says which list it is using.

The evening check. Every evening after Cboe publishes, the day's Scintilla equity line is stored beside Cboe's official PCCE and the index line beside PCCI, with the difference. After twenty sessions that comparison can say how closely our estimate tracks the real thing. Until then the line is called "the market-wide estimate" and never "the put/call ratio".

What cannot be had, said plainly. SPX index option volume does not come through the tick this reader uses, so the intraday index line is the index funds (SPY, QQQ, IWM), never SPX. For SPX itself the Station shows Cboe's daily PCSPX print, and labels it as the previous session.

Building one from the contracts instead is not practical: AAPL alone lists 25 expirations across 131 strikes — about 6,550 contracts — on one exchange of 41.

6 · What switches on when you say "token ok"

Nothing is being stored today. The whole path is built, tested and switched off, and the switch is a single random string:

  • you (or the coordinator) set that string as a Supabase secret and put the same string in this Mac's Keychain;
  • the reader finds it there and starts posting its readings to one door that can only append to the two put/call tables — no update, no delete, no price table;
  • everything it collected while switched off is already on disk, and one --flush sends it, resuming rather than re-sending;
  • the token is never written to disk, never logged, and never leaves the Keychain. The database's own key stays inside Supabase and never touches this Mac.

Twenty stored sessions is the point at which the gap between index funds and single names can say what "usual" looks like for our names. Every day without the switch is a day later that arrives.

7 · What could be wrong

  • The two bars are not on your screen yet. The code that keeps the volumes is written and tested, but the chart API has to be deployed and its collector re-run before the stored series carries them. Until then the pane shows exactly what the last screenshot pair shows: the ratio, and a line saying it is waiting.
  • Only sessions from 2019-10-07 will ever have the two amounts. Cboe published the ratio alone before that. The pane says so instead of drawing a zero bar.
  • The statistics answer a 20-year question about Cboe's numbers, not about ours. Our own per-name gap has no history at all until the switch above is thrown.
  • The narrow-gap finding could be luck. It is one of twenty-four measurements. It is written here as interesting, not as a rule to trade.
  • Forward returns are SPY close to close and ignore dividends and any cost of trading.
