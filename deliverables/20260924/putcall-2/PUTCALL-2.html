Put and call — two things, not one fraction

What the put/call number is made of, what Scintilla can honestly measure of the whole market, and the gap between index funds and single names.

Scintilla · M50 · 24 September 2026 · written for Alan, not for an engineer

1. The fraction, in plain words

A put/call ratio is one number made of two. Put it this way and it stops being slippery:

Say it once

A lower number means fewer puts per call.

  • 0.50 — one put for every two calls.
  • 1.00 — one put for every call.
  • 1.20 — twelve puts for every ten calls.

So yes: lower means fewer puts.

You said the fractions are hard because several things can move them. That is exactly right, and it is why a single number is a bad way to show this. 0.81 can rise for two opposite reasons: people bought puts, or people stopped buying calls. One is fear arriving. The other is enthusiasm leaving. The number looks identical either way.

So every place a put/call appears now shows three things: how many puts traded, how many calls traded, and the ratio beside them — plus one line saying which side actually moved.

Why “which side moved” is measured carefully

The reader cannot watch 364 names at once — Interactive Brokers allows 101 live lines on your account — so it rotates. That means the set of names it has just read is not always the same set it read at the open. If a name with cheap puts drops out of the count, the group ratio jumps with nothing having been traded. The movement line therefore compares only the names read at both moments, and the pane is told when the roster changed. There is a test that holds this in place.

Funds are kept out of sectors deliberately. SPY is filed under “Financial Services” in the profile table, and letting it sit there would drop the whole market’s hedging volume into one sector and make that sector look terrified.

Volumes are summed first and divided once. Averaging each name’s ratio would let a tiny name with two puts and one call count as much as Apple.

3. “The actual put/call ratio” — the market-wide estimate

You were right that what we had was an internal ratio: the 364 names Scintilla carries. Here is what it would take to speak about the whole market, and what it honestly costs.

How long a sweep takes

Measured on your own Gateway: 363 names in 121 seconds, and the account refused the 102nd simultaneous line. Everything below is arithmetic on that measurement.

The number that matters is not the total — it is that the oldest name in the reading is one rotation old. Widening the sweep does not make the line late; it makes it broader and slower, and the pane says which.

Which names to widen to

Two honest ways to pick the busiest optionable names, and the code says which one produced a given list:

  • Our own totals. Rank by the option volume the reader itself recorded over the last few sessions. No outside list needed, but it can only rank names we already watch.
  • A free public list. OCC publishes total US option volume daily; Cboe publishes its own. Either can seed a wider list, and both are free.

How much of the market that covers

Coverage is measured in contracts traded, not in names, because a handful of busy names carry most of the volume. Until a market total from OCC or Cboe is actually read in, the coverage figure refuses to print — it says “no market total supplied” rather than flattering itself with a percentage.

What it gets called

Until the evening comparison below has earned better, the line is called “the market-wide estimate”. Not “the put/call ratio”.

4. Checking it against Cboe, every evening

Cboe publishes the official ratios once a day, after the close — the equity one (PCCE) and the index one (PCCI). The Station already draws both back to 2006. Each evening the comparison stores one row per line: our closing reading, Cboe’s print, the difference, and how many names ours was measured on.

The two are not the same population: Cboe counts every option on its own exchanges; we sum the names our reader reaches. A steady offset is expected and is the useful part — if ours always sits, say, 0.88× Cboe’s, that is a working instrument. The page will print a sentence like “over 20 sessions the equity estimate sat 0.88× Cboe’s print; 17 of 19 day-to-day moves went the same way”. Two sessions is the minimum before anything is said at all; twenty before it means much.

The one thing that cannot be had: SPX

Your Gateway returns no option volume for the SPX index (it returns open interest, so the connection is fine — the volume simply is not published for the index itself). Summing SPX option contracts directly is not possible on this account: one expiry is hundreds of strikes and you have 101 lines. So, plainly:

  • There is no intraday SPX put/call from this Gateway.
  • For SPX, the Station shows Cboe’s published PCSPX — one print per session.
  • The index line is built from the funds (SPY, QQQ, IWM). That is a real, useful number, and it is labelled as the funds. It is never called SPX.

Index funds carry 0.55 more puts per call than single names. Everything together reads 0.81, which is why the single number hides the story.

What it means. Index funds are where portfolios buy protection — a fund manager hedges the whole book with SPY puts. Single names are where traders reach for upside — calls on Nvidia, calls on AMD. A wide gap means the market is hedged underneath while speculating on top.

It is not yet called wide. One reading cannot say what usual is. The gap is stored once per session, and nothing will be called unusual until there are 20 stored sessions — about a trading month — with 60 sessions, a quarter, before the shape is settled. Until then the page says how many days are still needed, rather than guessing.

6. What is waiting on the token

Nothing is being stored yet. The reader writes every reading to a file on your MacBook, and that file is the durable record. To get those readings into the database, one thing is needed:

  • A write-only ingest token — a random string. It goes in the Mac’s Keychain and in Supabase’s own settings. Holding it lets the reader append put/call readings and nothing else: it cannot read, cannot delete, and cannot touch a price table.
  • The door it knocks on is a small function called ibkr-ingest, written but not deployed. The database key stays inside Supabase. No key is ever copied onto your Mac.
  • Until the token exists the reader keeps spooling to disk. When it is switched on, the spooled sessions can be sent up and nothing collected in the meantime is lost.

Two new database tables hold the minute readings and the daily lines. They only add; nothing existing is changed, renamed or deleted, and a rollback is written for both.

8. What could be wrong

  • The volumes are running totals for the day. They only rise and they reset each morning. If anything ever reads a reset as a fall, the number would be nonsense — so a fall inside one session is refused and flagged rather than stored.
  • Coverage moves. A reading over 300 of 364 names is a different fact from one over 364. Every line carries both numbers; if a pane ever shows a ratio without them, do not trust it.
  • Our line is not Cboe’s line. Different population, so expect an offset. That is why it is called an estimate until measured.
  • The reader only runs while this MacBook is awake. That is the honest limit of collecting market data on a laptop.
  • The test data is synthetic. The maths is proven against hand-made readings shaped like IBKR’s, not against a recorded live session — the Gateway’s link was down when the fixtures were made.

9. What is not done

  • The pane is not built. The Station chart receives only the finished ratio from the chart API; it never receives the two volumes. Showing puts and calls as two bars needs the chart API to serve them alongside the ratio. The exact shape it must serve — puts, calls, ratio, how many names were measured, of how many, and the age of the oldest — is specified, and all the arithmetic behind it is written and tested. Nothing was written into the pane blind, because an unrendered pane is how blank boxes ship.
  • Nothing was stored, deployed or started. No database write, no function deployed, no schedule installed.
  • No live sweep was run tonight. The market was closed; the numbers quoted here are the coordinator’s measured readings from 02:36–02:40Z.
  • No screenshots. The tooling in this session could not launch a browser.

Nothing here was deployed. Nothing on Alan’s screen was touched.
